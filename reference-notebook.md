# Madison Plume’s reference notebook

Short explanations for readers of the Madison Plume comics. Each note links to the source used to check it. The app can show these notes directly or give the same notes to either local AI model for comparison.

## The Electoral College
<!-- ref: electoral-college -->

When you vote for president, your ballot helps choose electors. Each state gets one elector for each seat it has in Congress. Washington, D.C., gets three. In 48 states and D.C., the candidate with the most votes there gets all their electors. Maine and Nebraska give two electors to the statewide winner and one to the winner in each congressional district. The electors then vote for president and vice president. It takes a majority to win. With all 538 electoral votes, that’s 270.

- [U.S. National Archives · How the Electoral College works](https://www.archives.gov/electoral-college/about)
- [U.S. National Archives · How electoral votes are assigned](https://www.archives.gov/electoral-college/allocation)

## The three branches of government
<!-- ref: three-branches -->

Congress makes federal laws. The president leads the branch that carries them out. The federal courts explain what the laws mean and apply them in cases. Splitting the work helps keep any one branch from getting too much power. For example, the president can veto a bill passed by Congress, and Congress can override it with a two-thirds vote in each chamber.

- [USAGov · Branches of the U.S. government](https://www.usa.gov/branches-of-government)

## The Bill of Rights and the First Amendment
<!-- ref: bill-of-rights -->

The Bill of Rights is the first ten changes, or amendments, to the U.S. Constitution. It protects rights and sets limits on government power. The First Amendment protects religion, speech, the press, peaceful gatherings, and the right to ask the government to fix a problem.

- [U.S. National Archives · What the Bill of Rights says](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/bill-of-rights/what-does-it-say)

## Why the Federal Reserve is independent
<!-- ref: fed-independence -->

The Fed needs room to make interest-rate decisions based on the economy, even when those decisions are unpopular in an election year. Keeping rates too low for a quick boost can make it harder to keep prices stable later. Congress sets the Fed’s goals, including stable prices and as many people working as the economy can sustain. The Fed decides how to pursue those goals and explains its decisions to Congress and the public.

- [Federal Reserve Board · Why monetary policy is separated from political influence](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/why-is-it-important-to-separate-federal-reserve-monetary-policy-decisions-from-political-influence.htm)
- [Federal Reserve Board · Independence and accountability](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/about_12799.htm)

## Inflation and buying power
<!-- ref: inflation -->

Inflation means prices across the economy are rising over time. The same money buys less. One sandwich getting pricier could be due to something at that shop, so it takes a wider look to see inflation. The Consumer Price Index tracks how the cost of a basket of common goods and services changes. If inflation slows but stays above zero, prices on average still rise, just more slowly.

- [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics · Consumer Price Index questions and answers](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/questions-and-answers.htm)

## Opportunity cost
<!-- ref: opportunity-cost -->

Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best thing you give up when you choose. Say you have one free hour and choose a movie over a nap. If a nap was your next choice, the rest you would have gotten is the opportunity cost. You can spend money or time only once.

- [Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis · Examples of opportunity cost](https://www.stlouisfed.org/open-vault/2020/january/real-life-examples-opportunity-cost)

## Checking a headline or online source
<!-- ref: check-a-source -->

Pause before you share. Open the story and check who wrote it, when it was published, and what evidence it gives for the claim. Then open a new tab and see what other reliable sources say about the claim and the source behind it. Ten posts that all copy the same story still lead back to one source.

- [Digital Inquiry Group · Civic Online Reasoning](https://cor.inquirygroup.org/)
- [Associated Press · How AP checks online claims](https://www.ap.org/verify)

## Percent changes in headlines
<!-- ref: percent-change -->

Ask for the old number, the new number, and the time and place they cover. An increase from one theft to four thefts is up 300%. That’s three more thefts. The same percentage could describe a jump from 100 to 400. To check the math, subtract the old number from the new one, divide by the old number, and multiply by 100. A starting count of zero makes that percent change undefined.

- [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics · How to calculate a percent change](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/factsheets/calculating-percent-changes.htm)

## Cropped or mislabeled photos
<!-- ref: photo-context -->

A real photo can be shared with the wrong date, place, or caption. A crop can leave out something important. Try a reverse image search to find earlier or wider copies, then compare the captions and check who posted the original. Finding a match is a clue. Check whether the original photo actually supports the claim.

- [Associated Press · How AP verifies images and other online material](https://www.ap.org/verify)
