## The Electoral College
<!-- ref: electoral-college -->

Picture a relay race with a very long scorecard. When you vote for president, your ballot helps choose electors. Each state gets one elector for each seat it has in Congress. Washington, D.C., gets three. In 48 states and D.C., the candidate with the most votes in each gets the whole slate. Maine and Nebraska give two to the statewide winner and one to the winner in each congressional district. The electors then vote for president and vice president. Winning takes a majority. With all 538 electoral votes, that’s 270.

*All that and they still won’t give me a student discount.*

- [U.S. National Archives · How the Electoral College works](https://www.archives.gov/electoral-college/about)
- [U.S. National Archives · How electoral votes are assigned](https://www.archives.gov/electoral-college/allocation)

## The three branches of government
<!-- ref: three-branches -->

The Constitution gave this group project three job descriptions. Congress makes federal laws. The president leads the branch that carries them out. Federal courts explain what the laws mean and apply them in cases. Sharing the work helps keep too much power out of any one set of hands. For instance, the president can veto a bill Congress passes. Congress can override that veto with a two-thirds vote in each chamber.

*Put all three jobs on one name tag and you’ve got a king with a label maker.*

- [USAGov · Branches of the U.S. government](https://www.usa.gov/branches-of-government)

## The Bill of Rights and the First Amendment
<!-- ref: bill-of-rights -->

Think of the Bill of Rights as a note in the margin to the government. “Easy there.” These first ten amendments to the Constitution protect rights and limit government power. The First Amendment protects freedom of religion, speech, and the press. It also protects the right to gather peacefully and ask the government to fix a problem.

*I may petition for better coffee. The government may continue to serve what it calls coffee.*

## Why the Federal Reserve is independent
<!-- ref: fed-independence -->

Election Day comes with a deadline. The economy refuses to check the calendar. The Fed needs room to set interest rates based on the economy, even when its decisions are unpopular in an election year. Keeping rates too low for a quick boost can make it harder to keep prices stable later. Congress sets the Fed’s goals, including stable prices and as many people working as the economy can sustain. The Fed decides how to pursue those goals and explains its decisions to Congress and the public.

*If campaign promises could cool inflation, every debate would come with a complimentary sweater.*

- [Federal Reserve Board · Why monetary policy is separated from political influence](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/why-is-it-important-to-separate-federal-reserve-monetary-policy-decisions-from-political-influence.htm)
- [Federal Reserve Board · Independence and accountability](https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/about_12799.htm)

## Inflation and buying power
<!-- ref: inflation -->

Twelve bucks for a sad little sandwich? Take a breath. One shop might have its own reason for raising prices. Inflation means prices are rising across the economy over time, so the same money buys less. The Consumer Price Index tracks the changing cost of a basket of common goods and services. If inflation slows but stays above zero, prices on average still rise, just more slowly.

*At this price, the pickle should at least validate my parking.*

- [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics · Consumer Price Index questions and answers](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/questions-and-answers.htm)

## Opportunity cost
<!-- ref: opportunity-cost -->

You have one free hour. Movie or nap? If you pick the movie and the nap was your next choice, the rest you gave up is your opportunity cost. It’s the value of the next-best thing you could have done. You can spend that hour only once, just like a dollar. Your pillow may file an appeal.

*My pillow’s lawyer has requested a recess. Naturally.*

- [Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis · Examples of opportunity cost](https://www.stlouisfed.org/open-vault/2020/january/real-life-examples-opportunity-cost)

## Checking a headline or online source
<!-- ref: check-a-source -->

Give your share button a little nap. Open the story and check who wrote it, when it ran, and what evidence backs the claim. Then open a new tab and see what other reliable sources say about the claim and who made it. Ten posts copying the same story are one source with a very loud echo.

*The share button is very brave for someone who never has to issue a correction.*

- [Digital Inquiry Group · Civic Online Reasoning](https://cor.inquirygroup.org/)
- [Associated Press · How AP checks online claims](https://www.ap.org/verify)

## Percent changes in headlines
<!-- ref: percent-change -->

“Up 300%” is a number wearing a dramatic hat. Ask for the old count, the new count, and the time and place they cover. Going from one theft to four is up 300%. That’s three more thefts. Going from 100 to 400 is also up 300%, with 300 more thefts. For the math, subtract the old number from the new one, divide by the old number, and multiply by 100. A starting count of zero makes that calculation undefined.

*The percent sign did jazz hands. I still want to see the receipts.*

- [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics · How to calculate a percent change](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/factsheets/calculating-percent-changes.htm)

## Cropped or mislabeled photos
<!-- ref: photo-context -->

Sherlock Hound would ask, “What’s just outside the frame?” A crop can hide something important. Even a real photo can travel with the wrong date, place, or caption. Try a reverse image search for older or wider copies, compare their captions, and check who posted the original. A match is a clue. See if the original actually backs up the claim.

*Crop me right and I’m six feet tall. Crop me wrong and I’m a floating hat.*

- [Associated Press · How AP verifies images and other online material](https://www.ap.org/verify)

## How a bill becomes a federal law
<!-- ref: bill-to-law -->

A bill is a proposal for a law with a lot of desks in its future. A member of Congress introduces it, and a committee can review it. The House and Senate must pass the same text before it goes to the president. The president can sign it or veto it. Congress can override a veto with a two-thirds vote in each chamber. The Constitution also has rules for bills the president leaves unsigned.

*I got a paper cut just thinking about it. Please forward it to the appropriate committee.*

- [USAGov · How federal laws are made](https://www.usa.gov/how-laws-are-made)

## Federal and state powers
<!-- ref: federalism -->

Federalism gives the national government and the states different jobs. The Constitution gives Congress powers like coining money and regulating trade between states. Under the Tenth Amendment, the states or the people keep the powers left outside the national government’s authority and within the Constitution’s limits on the states. Some jobs overlap. Both levels can collect taxes, in case you were hoping one of them had misplaced your address.

*Apparently ‘I thought the other government was handling it’ is a poor tax strategy.*

## The Declaration of Independence and the Constitution
<!-- ref: founding-documents -->

In 1776 the colonies sent Britain a breakup letter with reasons attached. That was the Declaration of Independence. The Constitution, written in 1787, sets up the federal government and spells out its powers and limits. Starting a country and running one turned out to be two different assignments.

*Dear Britain, we’re seeing other forms of government.*

- [U.S. National Archives · America’s founding documents](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs)

## How the Constitution is amended
<!-- ref: amendment-process -->

Editing the Constitution takes more than a red pen. Two-thirds of each house of Congress can propose an amendment. Under Article V, two-thirds of state legislatures can also ask Congress to call a convention to propose amendments. Either way, three-fourths of the states must approve a change before it becomes part of the Constitution. The president has no formal role here. Save the fancy signing pen for another day.

*I once got a whole committee to agree on a comma. Reader, it was a very long lunch.*

- [U.S. National Archives · Constitutional amendment process](https://www.archives.gov/federal-register/constitution)

## How Fed interest rates affect borrowing
<!-- ref: interest-rates -->

A Fed rate cut can make some loans cheaper, which generally nudges people and businesses to borrow or spend more. Your loan agreement still has the calendar. It tells you when payments are due, and an existing fixed-rate loan follows the rate in its contract. Higher rates generally make new loans more expensive, so some purchases go back on the “maybe later” list.

*My dream kitchen is now a Pinterest board with excellent credit.*

- [Federal Reserve Board · How monetary policy works](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/monetary-policy-what-are-its-goals-how-does-it-work.htm)

## Supply, demand, and prices
<!-- ref: supply-demand -->

Supply is how much sellers will offer at different prices. Demand is how much buyers are willing and able to buy at those prices. Picture a concert. Same number of seats, more fans, and the ticket price tends to rise. Open up more seats while demand stays the same, and it tends to fall. Change one thing at a time and you can see what moved the price, even from the cheap seats.

*My wallet loves live music. It just wishes the band were less popular.*

- [Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis · Demand, supply, and market equilibrium](https://www.stlouisfed.org/~/media/education/tools/pdf/ea-tools-for-teaching.pdf)

## What GDP measures
<!-- ref: gdp -->

Gross domestic product, or GDP, adds up the value of final goods and services made inside a country over a period of time. Count the finished loaf, flour and all. The flour inside already got its credit. Real GDP adjusts for price changes so you can compare how much the economy made over time, even when the bread costs more.

*The flour called its publicist. It still only gets counted once.*

- [U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis · What to know about GDP](https://www.bea.gov/resources/learning-center/what-to-know-gdp)

## How the unemployment rate is counted
<!-- ref: unemployment -->

That big unemployment number comes with a checklist. In the main U.S. measure, a person generally counts as unemployed if they have no job, are available to work, and actively looked in the past four weeks. People on temporary layoff who expect to be called back can also count. The rate divides the unemployed count by the labor force, which includes employed and unemployed people. Someone who stopped looking usually falls outside that count.

*The checklist has a checklist. Somewhere, a clipboard is living its best life.*

- [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics · How the government measures unemployment](https://www.bls.gov/cps/cps_htgm.htm)

## Primary and secondary sources
<!-- ref: primary-sources -->

A diary from 1787 can put you in the room, at least on paper. It’s a primary source about life in 1787 because it gives direct evidence. A historian’s later article about the diary is a secondary source on that topic. Both can help. Ask what the writer could know, what they left out, and whether other evidence backs them up. Even a diary can be selective about the embarrassing bits.

*Future historians, please ignore the seventeen pages I wrote about a bad haircut.*

- [Library of Congress · Getting started with primary sources](https://www.loc.gov/programs/teachers/getting-started-with-primary-sources)

## News reporting and opinion
<!-- ref: news-opinion -->

Same paper, two different jobs. A news report aims to tell you what happened and how the reporter knows. An opinion piece makes a case for what the writer thinks. A good argument can use solid facts, and reporting can quote people’s opinions. Look for a label like Opinion or Editorial, then ask what the piece is mainly trying to do. The size of the headline is a poor clue.

*The news can report that I wore the hat. The editorial is free to call it a public nuisance.*

- [News Literacy Project · Information zones](https://newslit.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/InfoZones3-1.pdf)

## Misleading chart axes
<!-- ref: chart-axes -->

Before a chart makes you gasp, read the numbers and units on its axes. Start the scale at 90, and bars for 95 and 100 can look miles apart. You’re seeing only the tops of the bars. Start them at zero and their lengths show the sizes fairly. A line chart can zoom in to show a small change if it labels the scale clearly.

*A bar chart can turn a speed bump into the Alps. Pack a ruler.*

- [UK Office for National Statistics · Axes and gridlines](https://service-manual.ons.gov.uk/data-visualisation/guidance/axes-and-gridlines)

## Mean and median
<!-- ref: mean-median -->

Put 2, 3, and 100 in a line. The median is the middle number, 3. With two middle numbers, take their mean. To find the mean of the whole list, add the numbers and divide by how many there are. Here it’s 35. One big number can pull the mean away from what looks typical. In this lineup, 100 brought a megaphone.

*If a billionaire joins my book club, our average net worth soars. My debit card remains unmoved.*

- [National Institute of Standards and Technology · Measures of location](https://itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda351.htm)
